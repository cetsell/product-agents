# The Lindy PM Standard of Performance

*By Flo | 2024.01.01*

This is the “standard of performance” we wrote for PMs at Lindy, lightly edited for external consumption. If you’re here for very tactical advice, jump straight to **Holding the Bar**.

---

## About This Document

This is going to be a long doc!  
It’s inspired by Bill Walsh’s excellent “The Score Takes Care of Itself”, which exhorts you to focus on your inputs. If you do that, and get every single one of these, even the most minute, exactly right, then the output (“the score”) will take care of itself.

It follows that you should be **excruciatingly clear** with everyone on what exactly is expected of them. It’s hard enough to do a good job when you know exactly what your job is; it’s impossible if that was unclear to begin with.

So, this document will lay out:

- Your high-level mission — the output we’re going after
- The activities you’ll perform in pursuit of this mission — the input
- What your first few weeks will look like

---

## Mission

> **Ship a “holy shit” product** that, long-term, brings us closer to our vision of the AI employee and, short term, builds a growing business.

## Responsibilities / Activities

You must ensure that we (in this order):

1. **Build the right thing**
2. **Build it right**
3. **Build it fast**

### Build the Right Thing

This is first on the list for a reason — nailing execution doesn’t matter if you’re working on the wrong thing.

To “build the right thing,” you must first build a solid point of view around:

- The category we are playing in (no-code workflow automation)
- The opportunity that already existed within it before AI
- How it is impacted by recent changes in AI
- Which new use cases can be served?
- How can existing ones be served better?
- The state of the union at Lindy
  - What’s working? What isn’t?
- The most important things to work on next
  - What must **urgently** be fixed in order to bridge our “performance gap?”
  - What must **importantly** be built in order to bridge our “opportunity gap?”

> These two terms were coined by HBS professor Linda Hill. 

**In a nutshell:**
- Bridging your *performance gap* means taking care of your existing business; e.g., if you’re Apple in 2000, your performance gap is the Mac. How do you keep taking market share in the PC industry?
- Bridging your *opportunity gap* means pursuing the things your business could become. For Apple in 2000, this was the iPod.
- **You never run out of performance gap to bridge, but you must still find a way to bridge the opportunity gap — because if you don’t do it, someone else will, and it will become your performance gap.**

#### How to Build This Point of View

- **Direct exposure:** Spend a ton of time in the product to automate workflows of varying complexity.
    - Start small and gradually build more and more powerful Lindies. It’s important to stretch the product and get a first-hand feel for its limits.
    - As you use the product, keep a detailed "friction log" ([see explainer](https://uxdesign.cc/friction-logs-a-simple-tool-for-better-product-development-e49b1dfddee)). Share it with the team at the end of your first week.
- **Conversations** with X, Y, and me — the people closest to the customer.
- **Meeting with customers**, especially our top ones:
    - **Day 1, Mission #1:** Get the list of top 20 customers from X.
    - Build a Lindy to send them invites, 3 reminders over a week if no reply, and if they do reply and say yes, cc your scheduling Lindy to find times.
    - Tag Flo along (I love meeting users) and invite others as preferred.
    - **What you’re trying to learn:**
        - Who are they? 
        - What problem are they trying to solve?
        - Why us? What else have they tried? How did it fall short? How did they hear about us?
        - What do they like so far? What do they dislike?
- **Meeting with prospective customers:**  
    - Ask X to add you to her implementation calls.  
    - X is our top power user and a great salesperson.  
    - Take notes and be ready to share them at the next all-hands.
- **Answering support tickets and messages in our Slack community.**
    - Spend lots of time in the community.  
    - The goal: everyone knows you by name!
    - Ask X to be added to the customer support rotation.

**Throughout this:**
- Ask lots of dumb questions.
- Talk about the ugly stuff — it’s hard to solve problems if we don’t talk about them.
- Take a lot of notes and share them.

### Build it Right

“Build it right” comes before “build it fast” for two reasons:

1. *We don't want to ship half-baked products to see what sticks.*
2. *Customers care more about quality than speed: if your product is crap, nobody cares how fast you shipped the crap. ([More thoughts here](https://link-to-more-thoughts.com)).*

**Building the product right comes through a combination of 4 critical ingredients:**

#### 1. Taste

This is the most important component — and often underrated by PMs.  
Being “data-driven” is important but can never be a substitute for judgment or taste.

- Some of the most data-driven companies (Google, Amazon) also build some of the worst products. Meanwhile, the best products come from companies that don’t even have much usage data internally (unnamed, but you know who).
- *As David Hume argued in “The Standard of Taste,” taste is a skill like any other, and can be refined.*

**Suggestions:**
- Listen to your inner voice. Does what you’re shipping make your heart sing?
- Pay attention to the details. “God is in the details,” — Steve Jobs.
- Notice (and use!) other great products. Share cool details you spot in #design Slack.
- Maximize contrast. When making product decisions, think in extremes. E.g., a product with many settings vs. none (better to err towards fewer).
- Dare to be opinionated. The 5 most dangerous words in product: “let’s run an A/B test.”  
  (OK for optimization, not qualitative decisions — use intuition honed by experience.)

#### 2. Exposure to Users

Jared Spool’s research:  
> “The solution? Exposure hours. The number of hours each team member is exposed directly to real users… There is a direct correlation between this exposure and improvements in the designs.”

- Spend time not just talking with users, but **watching them use the product!**

> The deeper reason: Exposure to users helps you build a better mental model of the user, which means you’re able to make decisions faster without needing to check every time.

#### 3. Deep Understanding of the Field

- Build a “strong mental model of the domain.”
- Understand the technology cold.
- If you don’t understand a term in engineering meetings, ask or research.
- (Use your favorite LLM!)

#### 4. Iteration

- Tight iteration loops can sometimes substitute for product genius (see: natural selection).
- You need to try, see shortcomings, make changes, rinse and repeat.
- **Recommended:** Watch Tom Chi’s “Rapid Prototyping” talk.
    - Favorite idea: *"Stay close to the medium."*
        - Painters work directly in their medium. If you had to make bureaucratic moves for every brushstroke, masterpieces wouldn’t happen.
- *This is why we love the “ship by Friday” ritual: get to a playable version, even if rough, within a week for momentum and morale.*

#### 5. High Expectations

- “90% of doing great work is simply deciding to.”  
    - This is called the **Pygmalion Effect**: high expectations → high performance.

> Such a good tweet, I wonder who posted it.

**This makes the PM job almost easy: all you have to do is hold the bar!**

## Holding the Bar

This is so important it deserves its own section outlining what it means at each stage of the product lifecycle:

---

### 1. Product Jams

When the bones of the product are being thought through:

- Ask: Are we thinking big enough? Considered all options? Do we truly understand the job to be done?
    - _“Okay is terrible. You want great, you want fantastic, you want wow.”_ — Frank Slootman
    - The biggest blocker to “wow” is self-limitation.
    - Our value: **Refuse to accept constraints** (inspired by Paypal).

The product jam kicks off with the most important part: *aligning on the job to be done.*
- As Steve Jobs said:  
    > “Design is the fundamental soul...expressing itself in successive outer layers.”  
  The job to be done is the soul; everything else is downstream.

---

### 2. Design Reviews

Where we decide exactly what to build.  
Ask yourself: **Have we crafted the details?**

- The design review is decision-dense and very early in the lifecycle—changes are cheap here.
    - _"You can use an eraser on the drafting board, or a sledgehammer on the construction site."_ — Frank Lloyd Wright

#### During these reviews:

- Insist on **reviewing whole journeys**, not just screens—we design experiences.
- Try to simulate the user’s experience—click on prototypes, don’t just look at designs.
- **Demand real data** in designs (also means real copywriting; designers own this).
- **Try prototypes yourself** (designers won’t be in the room with users!).
- Listen to your inner voice. Notice confusion (multiply by 100x for users) and leave Figma comments before you forget.
- **Ask for multiple explorations:** The odds of nailing it first try are slim; trade-offs are clearer when comparing options.
- **Sweat the details:** Margins, colors, consistency, nitpicking is OK—the job isn’t to be popular. It’s to ship “holy shit” products.
- **Talk about the big things first:** Save minor/cosmetics feedback for last. Did the flow work? Are we missing something big? Avoid bikeshedding.
- *Designs presented must have ALL:*
    1. Multiple explorations
    2. A proper Figma prototype
    3. Real data, not placeholders

If anything’s missing, **adjourn** and reconvene once ready to make A+ decisions.

---

### 3. Product Reviews

For work in-progress or about to be shipped (needs product sign-off).

- **Team meetings:** Everyone with something to show presents.
    - This makes it feel higher-stakes (as it should).
    - Allows the team to learn from each other and distribute quality standards.

#### Running product reviews:

- Before: Ensure all DRIs will present.
- During: Insist on trying the product rather than just demos.
    - Watching someone skilled can make things look easy; only by trying it yourself do you find issues.

You are the **keeper of product quality!** The buck stops with you.

**What to look out for:**

- Copy
- Margins
- Text sizes
- Border radii
- Colors
- Animations
- Zero states
- Error states
- Loading states

*(This is a literal list: check each one.)*

- Take copious notes; record everything that should be fixed.
- Refer to these notes at the start of the next meeting to verify all were addressed.

#### Daily:

- Use Lindy every day. File tickets for every issue—small ones get the **Just Do It** tag, those fixable within 7 days get **Fit & Finish Friday**.
- Two utilities to help:
    - **Retroclip**: Record your screen, easily save video for tickets.
    - **CleanShot**: Annotate screenshots for clear feedback.

*One common failure mode:*  
You catch issues but never fix them.

- Why? After a long project, teams want to move on.
- Solutions:
    1. **Catch when this mistake happens:** Roadmap review is the moment—flag unfinished business before moving on.
    2. **Help the team prioritize “Fit & Finish” tickets:** File them, snooze in Linear to follow up, shield the team from distractions, hold them accountable until the notes are cleared.

### Build it Fast

This is the “project manager” side of the role (often owned by each project’s DRI). We have no ego about doing this—it’s all part of shipping a “holy shit” product.

**To help us move fast:**

- **Divide projects into milestones**—none longer than 2 weeks.
- **Keep everyone focused on hitting the next milestone**—mention the milestone date frequently.
    - “Do we still think we can make Jan 12th?”
    - If not, adjust *now*:
        - Remove distractions (e.g., offload engineer on-call)
        - Accept longer timeline (but debrief and learn why)
        - Cut the scope *(rare, but if you must, check that the essential job-to-be-done is preserved!)*

- **Help prioritize bugs.** [What We Should Fix When](#) (link to internal bug triaging thoughts)
- **Drive urgency.** ["Speed as a Habit"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/speed-habit-dave-girouard/):
    - Speed is a daily, repeated commitment.
    - Don’t let async morale drag things out: pull people into a quick room meeting if a discussion is stuck.
    - Bring follow-ups and blockers into Slack, rather than Gdocs/Figma/Linear/email.
    - If you notice confusion, hash it out in person. Whiteboards > back-and-forth threads. Then document and share.
    - Challenge: If a large meeting is scheduled way out, can it be a quick huddle today instead?
    - Decline meetings that lack agenda or efficiency.

---

### Bringing Order to the Chaos

- **Document every key decision**—keep a single source of truth for each project.
    - Big decisions happen everywhere (meetings, ad hoc syncs, Slack threads). Write them, circulate them, ensure follow-through.
- **Ensure resource accessibility:**  
    - All project resources should be linked in the project’s Slack header or bookmarks.
    - If someone searches for something and can't find it, add it next time.

- **Set lots of reminders** for everything you care about.
    - The downside of async is things fall through the cracks.  
    - Andy Grove: The only difference between delegation and abandonment is **monitoring**.
        - If someone drops the ball, it’s their fault; if you don’t notice, it’s yours.
    - Every “open loop” (Slack, Gdoc, Linear, Figma comment) should generate a reminder in your personal todo.
        - You’ll likely create ~10 reminders per day if you’re diligent.

## Your First Few Weeks

### Week 1

- Get setup
    - Complete everything on the onboarding checklist
- Create a couple of Lindies of your own
- Keep a friction log and share with the team at week’s end
- Get the list of top 20 customers from X
- Set up a Lindy for customer outreach (send invites, follow up if no reply, schedule times)
- Join the customer support rotation, answer 20+ tickets
- Join the Lindy community, answer 20+ questions
- 1:1s with: …
- Set up shadowing sessions for X’s implementation calls

### Week 2

- Have meetings with first customers
    - Invite Flo to all of them
    - Take plenty of notes & share with team
- Shadow more calls with X
- Lead the “X” project
    - Simple project, mostly working with X for designs & with Y (DRI) for execution—ensure it ships this week
- Write and publish your first changelog
- Help write documentation for the phone calls project

### Week 3

- Lead your first product review (if ready!)
- Define & implement a plan to measure user satisfaction on an ongoing basis
    - What question do we ask? Who do we ask it to? At what point of their journey? Via what channel?
